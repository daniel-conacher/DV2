Community gardens across Australia

Growing more than food

Australia's community gardens are spread unevenly, from school plots in Tasmania to five-hectare farms in Newcastle. Surveys show they are getting busier, giving away more food and leaving most members feeling better.

1Where are the gardens?

The national directory lists 624 community gardens, and 566 of them have a location we can map. They cluster along the east coast and around the capital cities.

Victoria has the most listings (204), followed by New South Wales (149). Tasmania's 95 listings include 50 school gardens, shown in purple.

Hover over a dot to see the garden's name, type and suburb. Use the dropdown to zoom to a state.

2Where are gardens most common?

Counting gardens per 100,000 residents changes the picture. Tasmania lists 16.5, more than four times any other state. Even without its school gardens it lists 7.8.

Queensland lists just 0.8. That is partly a gap in the directory, as section 9 shows.

Inside the big cities, inner councils lead: Port Phillip (10.5), Yarra (9.9) and the City of Sydney (7.0). In Hobart, Glenorchy reaches 23.8. Some remote councils look dark only because one garden among a few thousand people produces a high rate, so read those with care.

3How big are they?

Only 153 gardens reported their size. Of those, half are smaller than 700 m², about the size of a suburban house block, and a quarter are under 250 m².

A few city farms are far larger: Kooragang City Farm in Newcastle covers 5 hectares and Northey Street City Farm in Brisbane 2.5 hectares. The circle area shows garden size.

4Gardens are getting busier

In 2023 only 2 surveyed gardens had more than 100 visitors a week. In 2024, 17 did.

The bars show the share of responding gardens in each band, with 2023 on the left and 2024 on the right. Shares are used because the number of responses changed between years. Switch the measure to see that most gardens still have 11–50 members.

5What gardeners get back

Every year since 2022, more than 80% of gardeners have said their health and wellbeing improved after joining.

In 2024, 85.6% reported better wellbeing, 83.7% said they had learned to grow food at home, and 69% said they spend less on food.

6Feeding the neighbourhood

The share of gardens giving produce away to food banks, soup kitchens and charities rose from 25% in 2021–22 to a third in 2023. CGA's 2023 report links the rise to cost-of-living pressure.

Gardens are also social places. In 2024, 70% ran workshops, about half held community events and 48% shared meals in the garden. Each row below links one activity's survey results across the years.

7Low-impact by design

Low-impact growing is close to universal: 93% of gardens mulch, 82% improve their soil and 78% compost.

The one real shift is organic growing. The share of gardens strongly encouraging it fell from 61% to 50%. Darker cells mean more gardens.

Nine in ten gardens mulch their beds every year. The one real shift is fewer gardens strongly encouraging organic growing.

8Running on goodwill

82% of gardens now fund themselves through plot fees, plant sales and sausage sizzles, up from 73% in 2021–22. 59% receive government grants.

The gap is paid help. 60% still report unmet funding needs, and funding for paid staff fell from 16% of gardens in 2022 to about 4%. Bars show 2024 and black ticks show 2021–22.

9Reading the data carefully

These are survey and directory data, so they come with limits:

  • The surveys are self-selected: gardens choose to answer, so the results describe responding gardens, not all gardens.
  • The mix of states changes each year. New South Wales made up 36% of responses in one survey and 21% in another, which can shift national figures.
  • The directory is a 2018 snapshot and misses many gardens. Brisbane City Council lists 49 gardens, but only 7 of them appear in the directory.
